People & Community · Estimates to 2024

In Nepal, girls caught up.

Read the story + explore sources

The story

For girls in Nepal, finishing the stage after primary school was once much less common than for boys. UNESCO’s GEM team reports 1990 timely completion estimates equivalent to 8 in 100 girls and 41 in 100 boys, allowing a few extra years beyond the expected finishing age. Its 2026 account reports a 2024 estimate of 89 in 100 for both girls and boys.

Education support expanded over that period. The World Bank’s account of Nepal’s School Sector Development Program describes targeted scholarships and improvements intended to help disadvantaged children stay in school. UNESCO also describes free textbooks through grade 12, expanded provision of later school grades, and improvements to school water and sanitation as parts of the wider effort.

Why it matters

The hopeful change concerns who gets to finish a school level. A disadvantage that once separated girls’ and boys’ completion rates is no longer visible in the rounded national lower-secondary estimates. That is meaningful progress, even while some children remain excluded.

It is also unfinished: UNESCO estimates upper-secondary completion at 44% for girls and 53% for boys. Equal rates at one level do not establish equality throughout education.

Evidence and limits

The estimates refer to 2024; the central reporting was published in 2026. These are national estimates, not records of 100 pictured children. Lower-secondary completion is distinct from enrolment, grades, learning outcomes and completing all of school.

UNESCO distinguishes timely completion, assessed among people 3–5 years above the intended completion age, from ultimate completion, which allows longer delays. The country narrative and figure introduce both measures. The carousel uses the GEM team’s explicitly paired timely estimates for girls and boys in 1990 and its separately dated, rounded 2024 findings. It does not use the country page’s different 13% ultimate estimate as the girls’ baseline, and it does not reconstruct an annual series.

The underlying linked country table could not be retrieved in this check. No numerical uncertainty intervals or reconstructed trend are claimed. The policy accounts support the existence of education efforts; they do not isolate the causal contribution of any one intervention. National averages can conceal disadvantage within groups. Programme reporting by a funder is not an independent impact audit.

Sports

He built a knee to race.
Now his rivals use his designs.

Read the story + explore sources

The story

Mike Schultz wanted to race again. After a snowmobile racing injury in 2008 led to an above-knee amputation, he found that the prosthetic equipment he had tried did not suit the demands of his action sports. Drawing on his mechanical experience, he began developing a knee of his own.

His design used suspension principles: a spring-and-shock system could help manage impact and assist extension, with resistance adjusted to the rider and activity. In his account for The Players’ Tribune, Schultz describes testing a prototype at a qualifier, refining it and then winning silver at the 2009 Summer X Games.

In 2010, he founded BioDapt to make sports prosthetic components for other people. He went on to compete in Para snowboarding alongside athletes using his company’s designs—including his rivals. This was a business as well as an athlete’s contribution to the equipment of his sport.

After banked-slalom bronze at the 2026 Paralympics, Schultz retired from competitive snowboarding. BioDapt and software company Autodesk have announced work toward prosthetics for athletes targeting the Los Angeles 2028 Paralympics. His next chapter carries the same thread as his first invention: applying what he learns to help other riders take part.

Why it matters

An idea developed for one athlete became equipment used by others, including people competing against him. The hopeful part is practical: a rider’s experience informing tools that other athletes can use to pursue sports they love. It is not a claim that equipment alone produces medals or that access is already universal.

Evidence and limits

The early racing account comes from Schultz’s own retrospective. The IPC interview describes his equipment and its commercial role; AP reporting via MPR provides additional reporting on fellow competitors using it. Manufacturer descriptions support design principles, not independently measured superiority over other products.

IPC results record banked-slalom bronze on 13 March 2026, and U.S. Ski & Snowboard’s April retrospective confirms retirement from competitive snowboarding. The earlier AP article preceded that final medal, which explains its different career medal total.

BioDapt is a commercial manufacturer; this story does not establish donations, universal affordability or access. The pre-Games forecast of around 25 users is not a verified attendance count. The Autodesk collaboration describes future development aims, not benefits already delivered for 2028.

Nature & Environment

Once gone from its only wild home.
Now about 10,000 live free.

Read the story + explore sources

The story

The Christmas Island blue-tailed skink is a small, striped lizard with a vivid blue tail. Christmas Island was its only native home. Introduced predators drove the remaining wild population toward disappearance, making a rescue and breeding programme critical to its survival.

Sixty-six skinks collected in 2009–10 became the founders of conservation-breeding populations on Christmas Island and at Taronga Zoo. Keeping the species alive in captivity made another step possible: finding places where descendants could live and breed freely.

Parks Australia and its partners released skinks onto Pulu Blan in 2019 and Pulu Blan Madar in 2020. These are two islands in the Cocos (Keeling) group, outside the species’ original range. There were setbacks: invasive yellow crazy ants were linked to a decline on the second island. Ant control was followed by signs of recovery and breeding.

By September 2026, Taronga reported an estimated 4,000–5,000 skinks on each of those islands. A separate release of 545 animals onto Horsburgh, another island in the same group, created a further refuge opportunity. Long-term survival and breeding there still need follow-up.

Why it matters

A rescue involving just dozens of animals has helped keep a species alive and build free-living populations in new refuges. That is a meaningful conservation gain. It also shows why recovery takes continuing work: breeding, habitat assessment, threat control and monitoring, rather than a single release or a declaration that the species is saved.

Evidence and limits
What does “about 10,000” mean?The 2026 translocation application estimates approximately 5,000 skinks on each of the two earlier refuge islands. Taronga’s 21 September 2026 update gives a range of 4,000–5,000 per island: roughly 8,000–10,000 across both. The cover uses a rounded programme estimate, not an exact count, an independently audited census or a whole-species total. Captive animals and the 545 released on Horsburgh are not added to it.

The early trials needed continuing care. Pulu Blan Madar suffered a decline associated with yellow crazy ants; Parks Australia reported ant baiting and signs of recovery in 2021. The 2026 application describes the two earlier populations as self-sustaining. A 2024 Parks Australia letter reported lower estimates—over 4,000 on Pulu Blan and over 1,400 on Pulu Blan Madar. These different dated estimates are not a standardized population time series.

A third refuge is a new step.Parks Australia and Taronga report 545 skinks released on Horsburgh Island in 2026. Horsburgh is around 50 times the area of either earlier refuge island, offering more potential habitat. Long-term survival and breeding there still need follow-up. Introduced predators continue to prevent a return to Christmas Island; refuge populations do not mean the species has recovered in its original wild habitat.

The official accounts were published on 8 July and 21 September 2026. DAFF places releases in June; other reporting refers to July or June–July. We use “2026” for the release rather than treating an announcement date as the event date. Current accounts retain the IUCN category “Extinct in the Wild”, which can include free-living populations well outside a species’ past range; Australia’s national listing is Critically Endangered.

Health · October 2026

Born deaf.
A new possibility.

Read the story + explore sources

The story

Abdu and his younger sister Sumaya were born with profound hearing loss linked to changes in OTOF, a gene involved in passing sound signals from the inner ear to the hearing nerve. It helps cells make otoferlin, a protein needed for that communication.

At Cincinnati Children’s, Abdu received an investigational gene therapy in April 2025, when he was three. The approach delivers a working copy of OTOF into the inner ear so cells can make functional otoferlin. It adds genetic instructions; it does not edit the children’s existing DNA.

In its October 2026 update, the hospital reported that Abdu had detected sounds within four weeks and later heard within the normal range. Sumaya also received treatment and her hearing improved. The hospital did not report normal-range hearing for her, so the two outcomes should not be treated as identical.

The treatment, now called Otarmeni, received U.S. accelerated approval on 23 April 2026 for a specifically defined group of people with OTOF-related hearing loss. The siblings’ treatment and the later regulatory milestone belong to the same developing field, but they are different pieces of evidence.

Why it matters

The progress is a way to address a particular cause of hearing loss by helping the inner ear pass on sound signals. For these siblings, the hospital reports meaningful changes. For the wider field, it is a carefully bounded step toward more options—not a treatment for every form of deafness or a reason to assume everyone will have the same outcome.

Evidence and limits

The family account is a Cincinnati Children’s press release, not an independent audit of the children’s clinical records. The wider clinical trial was funded by Regeneron. Individual hospital updates and trial-group analyses should not be added together as separate, independent proof.

The FDA label describes a single-arm study: 24 participants were treated, with 20 in the week-24 efficacy analysis. Sixteen of those 20 reached an average pure-tone hearing threshold of 70 dB HL or better. That is a defined study endpoint, not a finding that 80% achieved normal hearing. For bilateral treatment, the better treated ear was used; one missing week-24 assessment was counted as non-response.

The approved indication requires molecularly confirmed variants in both copies of OTOF, severe-to-profound or profound hearing loss, preserved outer-hair-cell function, and no prior cochlear implant in the treated ear. Treatment involves surgery and carries procedure and treatment risks. Accelerated approval is based on hearing-sensitivity results; confirmatory evidence of clinical benefit is still required.
